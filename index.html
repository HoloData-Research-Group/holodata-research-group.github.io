Mission

HoloData Research Group investigates the ideas, methods, and mathematical structures that shape modern computing. Our work is centered on machine learning, deep learning, and quantum computing, with a particular interest in the questions that sit beneath rapidly changing technologies: how useful representations are formed, why learning systems generalize, how mathematical structure can guide model design, and where new computational paradigms may change what is possible.

We approach these questions as a research group rather than as advocates for any single technique. A model is not interesting simply because it is large, a method is not valuable simply because it is new, and a quantum formulation is not meaningful simply because it uses the language of quantum computing. We are interested in understanding the conditions under which an approach works, the assumptions on which it depends, and the ways in which it fails. Our research combines theoretical investigation with careful experimentation. We develop and analyze algorithms, study the behavior of learning systems, construct computational experiments, and compare methods through transparent evaluation. This includes work on deep neural architectures, probabilistic models, scientific machine learning, optimization, representation learning, and emerging approaches to quantum information processing.

Where possible, we look for connections between fields: between differential equations and learned models, between geometry and representation, between physical intuition and computation, or between classical and quantum approaches to inference and optimization. We value results that are clear about their limitations as much as results that demonstrate strong performance. Reproducibility, well-defined questions, meaningful baselines, and honest interpretation are central to the way we work, because research advances through results that can be examined, repeated, extended, and disputed. HoloData supports independent study and research, as well as collaboration with researchers, students, and organizations whose problems raise genuinely interesting technical questions. Engagement with the wider research community helps us test our assumptions and keeps our work connected to important problems beyond a single project.

Our aim is to contribute thoughtful, rigorous work to advanced computing: work that clarifies difficult ideas, explores promising directions without unnecessary hype, and builds a stronger understanding of how intelligent and unconventional computational systems can be designed, analyzed, and understood.

Current Projects

PDE-derived Gaussian Process kernels

This project investigates how partial differential equations can be used to construct and understand kernels for Gaussian process models. The aim is to connect the flexibility of probabilistic learning with the structure provided by known scientific or physical systems.

  • Deriving kernels from differential operators and boundary conditions.
  • Studying how prior structure affects predictions and uncertainty.
  • Comparing PDE-informed kernels with standard Gaussian process approaches.

Vision Transformer benchmarking

This project evaluates vision transformers across controlled tasks and datasets, with a focus on understanding when their attention-based architecture provides a meaningful advantage over more established computer vision models.

  • Comparing performance across datasets, model sizes, and training conditions.
  • Measuring accuracy, computational cost, data requirements, and robustness.
  • Examining how architectural and training choices affect the results.
